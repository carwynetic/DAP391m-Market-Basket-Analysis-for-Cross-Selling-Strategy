Abstract
This study applies Market Basket Analysis to the Online Retail II dataset to identify
co-purchase patterns and generate cross-selling candidates in e-commerce. The pipeline covers
transaction preprocessing, basket construction, transaction encoding, Apriori and FP-Growth
comparison, association rule generation, rule filtering, visualization, and supplementary
basket-value checking. Instead of focusing on a single rule, the study generates rules that
satisfy predefined support, confidence, and lift thresholds, then interprets the top rules
through a combined view of lift, support, and confidence. A representative regression-
based robustness check is also conducted to examine whether baskets containing a high-lift
association are associated with higher average order value after controlling for basket-level
characteristics. The results indicate that association rules can reveal meaningful co-purchase
patterns and generate recommendation candidates, although their operational value should
be validated further in a live business setting.
Keywords: Market Basket Analysis, Association Rule Mining, FP-Growth, Apriori, Cross-
Selling, E-commerce, Regression Analysis
1
Introduction
While Market Basket Analysis (MBA) has been widely applied to discover co-purchase patterns
and generate association rules, many studies primarily stop at rule generation. Relatively few
studies further examine whether high-lift associations remain associated with basket-level value
after accounting for basket characteristics. Therefore, this study not only identifies product
associations for cross-selling recommendations but also investigates their value relevance through
a supplementary robustness check.
The rapid growth of digital commerce has increased product variety and intensified competition
among businesses. As supermarkets, retail stores, and e-commerce platforms generate large
volumes of transaction data, firms increasingly need data-driven methods to understand customer
purchasing behavior and improve business decision-making [1, 2]. Market Basket Analysis
(MBA) is a data mining technique used to discover associations among products within customer
transactions. By identifying frequent itemsets and association rules, MBA can help businesses
observe which products tend to be purchased together and form candidate ideas for cross-selling,
product recommendation, promotion planning, and inventory-related decisions [7, 8].

Association rule mining algorithms such as Apriori and FP-Growth are commonly applied
in MBA to extract purchasing patterns. These methods evaluate product relationships using
support, confidence, and lift, allowing businesses to measure both the frequency and strength
of association patterns [7]. However, many MBA studies stop at generating rules and ranking
them by metrics, while less attention is given to whether high-lift rules are also associated
with higher basket-level value. This creates a practical research gap: association rules may
identify co-purchase patterns, but high lift alone does not necessarily imply operational value or
independent revenue improvement.
Therefore, this study applies Market Basket Analysis to the Online Retail II dataset from
the UCI Machine Learning Repository, which contains transaction records from a UK-based
online retail business between 2009 and 2011 [9]. The paper does not only mine co-purchase
patterns; it also checks the value relevance of a representative high-lift association through
basket-level regression. The study addresses three research questions: (RQ1) which basket
association rules have the highest lift values while meeting minimum support and confidence
thresholds; (RQ2) which product pairs or itemsets appear as promising cross-selling candidates
based on co-occurrence and lift; and (RQ3) whether baskets containing a representative high-lift
association are associated with higher basket value after controlling for basket characteristics.
2
Related Work
Market Basket Analysis has been widely applied in retail and e-commerce to discover product
relationships from transaction data. By identifying items that frequently appear together in
the same basket, this technique can support candidate generation for cross-selling, promotion
planning, and inventory management [8]. In digital commerce, such data-driven approaches are
increasingly important because businesses need to understand purchasing behavior at scale [1, 2].
Association rule mining is the main technique used in Market Basket Analysis. Apriori finds
frequent itemsets through candidate generation and pruning, but it can be costly for large
datasets [4]. FP-Growth improves efficiency by mining frequent patterns without generating
candidates, making it suitable for larger transaction datasets [6]. Previous studies have shown
that these methods can reveal purchasing patterns [7, 8]. This study extends the standard
rule-generation workflow by adding threshold justification, product-level interpretation, and a
representative regression-based robustness check to avoid treating association metrics as direct
evidence of business impact.

Proposed Methodology
3.1
Problem Setting and System Overview
Given a retail transaction dataset, the proposed Market Basket Analysis pipeline aims to identify
co-purchase patterns and convert them into cross-selling hypotheses. Each invoice is treated
as one customer basket, and products within the same invoice are considered co-purchased
items. The main outputs are frequent itemsets, association rules, top-ranked rule candidates,
rule metric visualizations, and a supplementary regression-based check.

3.2
Data Construction and Basket Definition
The study uses the Online Retail II dataset, consisting of online_retail_09_10.csv and
online_retail_10_11.csv [9]. After concatenation, the raw dataset contains 1,067,371 rows
and 8 columns: InvoiceNo, StockCode, Description, Quantity, InvoiceDate, UnitPrice,
CustomerID, and Country. InvoiceNo is used as the basket identifier, while StockCode is used
as the item identifier. This basket definition is suitable for MBA because each invoice represents
a transaction containing one or more purchased products.
The raw data are cleaned to retain valid purchase transactions. Rows with missing Description,
cancelled invoices, invalid quantities or prices, and duplicate records are removed. CustomerID
is retained even when missing because customer identity is not required for basket-level asso-
ciation rule mining. After these steps, the dataset contains 1,007,913 valid purchase records.
Non-product StockCodes such as POST, DOT, M, C2, ADJUST, BANK CHARGES, AMAZONFEE, test
codes, and gift voucher codes are removed because they represent fees, manual adjustments, or
non-merchandise entries rather than retail products. After product-code cleaning, the cleaned
basket-item dataset contains 1,003,357 rows.
A monetary feature is created for basket-value analysis:
LineTotal = Quantity × UnitPrice.
(1)
The cleaned records are grouped by InvoiceNo to create a transaction list of unique
StockCode values and an invoice-level feature table containing ProductRevenue, TotalQuantity,
AvgUnitPrice, CustomerID, and Country.

3.3
Basket Filtering and Transaction Encoding
Only baskets with at least two unique products are retained because association rules require
an antecedent and a consequent in the form A ⇒B. This produces 36,308 baskets. Very large
baskets are then removed using the 99th percentile basket-size threshold. The threshold is set at
203 unique products, reducing the rule-mining dataset to 35,945 baskets. This threshold limits
the influence of unusually large wholesale-like baskets and reduces computational burden while
retaining most ordinary basket behavior.
The filtered transaction list is converted into a one-hot encoded transaction matrix using
TransactionEncoder from mlxtend [10].
Each row represents a basket and each column
represents a product. A value of True indicates that the product appears in the basket, and
False indicates absence.
3.4
Frequent Itemset Mining and Threshold Logic
Support is used to determine whether an itemset appears frequently enough to be considered for
rule generation:
support(A) = Number of baskets containing itemset A
Total number of baskets
.
(2)
Both Apriori and FP-Growth are run with min_support = 0.01. This threshold requires an
itemset to appear in at least 1% of baskets, which balances two needs: retaining patterns that
occur often enough to be interpreted and avoiding an excessive number of rare and unstable
itemsets.
Apriori is used as a classical baseline [4], while FP-Growth is selected for final
rule generation because it mines frequent patterns without repeated candidate generation and
therefore scales better for the final rule-mining step [6].
Association rules are first generated using min_confidence = 0.2 to create a broad candidate
pool. The final strong rule set uses support ≥0.01, confidence ≥0.4, and lift ≥2. The confidence
threshold requires that at least 40% of antecedent baskets also contain the consequent, while
lift ≥2 requires the association to be at least twice as strong as random co-occurrence. These

3.5
Association Rule Interpretation
A rule is represented as:
A ⇒B,
(3)
where A is the antecedent and B is the consequent. Confidence measures the conditional
probability that B is purchased when A is purchased:
confidence(A ⇒B) = support(A ∪B)
support(A)
.
(4)
Lift measures whether the relationship between A and B is stronger than random co-
occurrence:
lift(A ⇒B) = confidence(A ⇒B)
support(B)
.
(5)
The top 20 rules are interpreted using a combined view of lift, confidence, and support. Lift
is useful for detecting non-random co-occurrence, but it can be inflated when support is low;
therefore, support and confidence are also considered when discussing rule quality. To improve
interpretability, each StockCode is mapped back to its product description so that rules can be
reported as readable product relationships rather than product codes only.
3.6
Supplementary Regression-Based Check
The regression component is supplementary rather than the core evidence of business impact.
Its purpose is to check whether a representative high-lift rule remains associated with average
order value after controlling for basket-level characteristics. Because the dataset consists of
historical transaction logs rather than experimental data, the observed associations should not
be interpreted as causal revenue effects.
For a representative rule, rule_applied=1 if a basket contains all products in the rule and 0
otherwise. The dependent variable is AOV, measured by invoice-level product revenue. Control
variables include BasketSize, AvgUnitPrice, TotalQuantity, and Country. Extreme observa-
tions are removed using the 99.5th percentile thresholds of AOV, BasketSize, TotalQuantity,
and AvgUnitPrice. OLS models are estimated with HC3 robust standard errors [12, 13].
3.7
Artifact Packaging and Dashboard Outputs
The final outputs include cleaned basket-item data, basket-level features, frequent itemsets,
association rules, strong rules, top 20 ranked rules, visualizations, and dashboard-ready artifacts.
The dashboard provides an executive overview, rule explorer, bundle recommendation cards,
algorithm comparison, scenario-based revenue simulator, and exploratory regression summary.
These modules are intended to help users inspect association patterns and business hypotheses,

4
Experimental Setup
4.1
Dataset Setup
The experiment is conducted on the cleaned Online Retail II transaction data prepared in
Section 3. After preprocessing and product-code cleaning, the dataset contains 1,003,357 basket-
item records. The final rule-mining dataset contains 35,945 baskets after removing single-item
baskets, which cannot form A ⇒B rules, and filtering unusually large baskets using the 99th
percentile basket-size threshold to reduce outlier influence and computational burden.
4.2
Mining Parameters
Apriori and FP-Growth are evaluated using min_support = 0.01. Association rules are generated
with min_confidence = 0.2, and the final strong rule set is selected using support ≥0.01,
confidence ≥0.4, and lift ≥2. The top 20 rules are selected through a combined interpretation
of lift, confidence, and support as promising cross-selling candidates, not as confirmed business
outcomes.
4.3
Regression Setup
A representative high-lift rule is examined using basket-level regression. The dependent variable is
AOV, while the main explanatory variable is rule_applied. The control variables are BasketSize,
AvgUnitPrice, TotalQuantity, and Country. This analysis is used as a robustness check for
basket-value association, not as a causal estimate.
4.4
Implementation
All experiments are implemented in Python using pandas, NumPy, mlxtend, Matplotlib, and
statsmodels [10, 11].
SQL-style checks are used during exploratory analysis to inspect in-
voices, products, countries, and transaction quality. The main outputs include association_
rules_strong.csv, top_20_association_rules.csv, rule visualizations, and dashboard-ready
artifacts.

5
Results and Discussion
5.1
Mining Results
The final strong rule set contains 614 rules that satisfy support ≥0.01, confidence ≥0.4, and lift
≥2. The top 20 rules are examined using lift, confidence, and support together. As shown in
Figure 3, their lift values range from 38.6112 to 52.6464. These results answer RQ1 by identifying
the highest-ranked rules under the selected thresholds. However, high lift should be interpreted
as a strong co-purchase pattern, not as evidence of a successful cross-sell or direct business value.
Figure 3: Top 20 association rules ranked by lift.
5.2
Rule Metric Interpretation
Figure 4 shows the relationship among support, confidence, and lift for the strong rules. The
support values range from 0.0100 to 0.0372, confidence values range from 0.4004 to 0.9216, and
lift values range from 3.0246 to 52.6464. These metrics provide different views of rule quality:
support reflects frequency, confidence reflects conditional purchase probability, and lift reflects
association strength relative to random co-occurrence.
These results answer RQ2 by showing which product relationships appear as promising
recommendation candidates. The product-description mapping also improves rule interpretability
because business users can inspect product names rather than only StockCodes. However, rules
with high lift may still have limited operational value if they occur in a small number of baskets or
are not suitable for real recommendation settings. Therefore, support, confidence, and lift should
be interpreted together, and high lift alone should not be treated as evidence of a successful
cross-sell.

5.3
Regression-Based Robustness Check
Because the dataset consists of historical transaction logs rather than experimental data, the
observed associations should not be interpreted as causal revenue effects. A representative
rule-level regression check is therefore used only to examine whether one high-lift rule remains
associated with AOV after controls. The representative rule is:
22748, 22745 ⇒22746.
(6)
This rule has support of 0.0104, confidence of 0.7411, and lift of 52.6464. In the basket-level
data, 557 baskets contain the antecedent products, 409 baskets contain the full rule, and 148
baskets are recommendation candidates. The regression dataset contains 39,516 baskets before
outlier treatment and 38,869 baskets after removing extreme observations.
5.4
Regression Results and Business Interpretation
Table 2 and Figure 5 summarize the regression result for the representative rule. In the baseline
model, rule_applied has a positive and statistically significant coefficient of 295.3146. After
adding BasketSize, the coefficient drops to 26.3143 and becomes statistically insignificant. After
further controlling for AvgUnitPrice, TotalQuantity, and Country, the coefficient remains
insignificant and becomes negative.

5.5
Discussion and Dashboard Support
The results should be interpreted across three layers. First, the mining layer identifies high-lift
association patterns. Second, the metric layer shows that support, confidence, and lift should
be considered together. Third, the regression-based robustness check shows that association
patterns do not automatically imply independent AOV improvement.
Overall, high lift does not equal operational value, and co-purchase patterns do not imply
causation. The discovered rules are best viewed as recommendation hypotheses that require
further validation using live recommendation logs or controlled business experiments [14]. The
dashboard supports this cautious interpretation by allowing users to explore rules interactively,
inspect readable product names, compare Apriori and FP-Growth, and test scenario-based
assumptions as supplementary business planning tools.

Conclusion
This study applies Market Basket Analysis to the Online Retail II dataset to identify co-
purchase patterns and generate cross-selling candidates. The pipeline combines data cleaning,
basket construction, transaction encoding, Apriori and FP-Growth comparison, rule filtering,
visualization, and supplementary regression-based checking. The final strong rule set contains
614 rules, and the top-ranked rules show high lift values under the selected thresholds.
The findings suggest that association rule mining is useful for generating recommendation
hypotheses, but high-lift rules should not be treated as confirmed operational value. The
representative regression check shows that the baseline AOV difference is largely attenuated
after adding basket size, quantity, and price-related controls. Future work should validate these
rule-based recommendations using customer segments, live recommendation logs, or controlled
experiments.
